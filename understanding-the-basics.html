Understanding the Basics

أُسُسُ الدِّين

The words, concepts, and foundations of Islām — explained clearly for every Muslim

📖 Based on the Qurʾān and authenticated Sunnah — Ahl al-Sunnah wa'l-Jamāʿah

In matters of creed (ʿaqīdah), we affirm what Allāh affirmed for Himself and what His Messenger ﷺ affirmed — without addition or alteration.

This page summarises foundational beliefs agreed upon by orthodox Sunni scholars across the centuries. It does not engage in theological debate, reinterpretation, or speculative philosophy.

📖 Methodological Note — Creed (ʿAqīdah)

In matters of belief, this page follows the understanding of the early generations (al-Salaf al-Ṣāliḥ) — the Companions, their students, and the leading orthodox scholars of Ahl al-Sunnah wa'l-Jamāʿah. We affirm what is affirmed in the Qurʾān and authentic Sunnah, without philosophical reinterpretation, speculative theology, or sectarian argumentation.

Section One

Key Vocabulary

These are words you will hear repeatedly in khutbahs, lectures, and Islamic conversation. Understanding them unlocks a deeper connection with everything you hear and read.

The Deen & Faith ▼

الدِّين
Ad-Dīn
The Deen — The Way of Life
Dīn is often translated as "religion" but it means far more — it is the complete way of life ordained by Allāh, encompassing belief, worship, character, law, and conduct. Islām is the Dīn.
You will hear: "This is the Deen of Allāh" — meaning the complete way Allāh has prescribed for us to live.
الإِسْلَام
Al-Islām
Islām — Submission and Peace
Islām comes from the Arabic root s-l-m, meaning both peace (salām) and submission (istislām). Islām is the complete submission of one's will to Allāh. Allāh says in the Qurʾān: "Indeed, the religion in the sight of Allāh is Islām." (Āl ʿImrān 3:19)
You will hear: "Islām is a complete way of life" — referring to its comprehensive nature covering every aspect of existence.
الإِيمَان
Al-Īmān
Īmān — Faith and Belief
Īmān means faith — but in Islamic theology it is not merely belief in the heart. Scholars of Ahl al-Sunnah explain that Īmān consists of belief in the heart, declaration with the tongue, and action with the limbs. It increases with obedience and decreases with disobedience.
You will hear: "Increase your Īmān" — a call to strengthen your faith through worship, knowledge, and good deeds.
الإِحْسَان
Al-Iḥsān
Iḥsān — Excellence in Worship
Iḥsān is the highest level of the Deen — excellence and perfection in worship. The Prophet ﷺ defined it in a famous authentic narration: "Worship Allāh as if you see Him, for even though you do not see Him, He sees you." (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8)
You will hear: "Allāh loves those who have Iḥsān" — referring to excellence and sincerity in everything we do.
التَّقْوَى
At-Taqwā
Taqwā — God-Consciousness
Taqwā is one of the most important concepts in the Qurʾān — often translated as "fear of Allāh" but more precisely it means God-consciousness: being always aware that Allāh sees you, and allowing that awareness to guide every action. The Qurʾān commands it repeatedly: "O you who believe, have taqwā of Allāh."
You will hear this word in almost every khutbah — imams frequently open with "I counsel you to have taqwā of Allāh."
التَّوَكُّل
At-Tawakkul
Tawakkul — Reliance on Allāh
Tawakkul means complete reliance and trust in Allāh — not passivity, but taking the necessary means whilst placing full trust in Allāh for the outcome. Allāh says: "And whoever relies upon Allāh — then He is sufficient for him." (al-Ṭalāq 65:3)
You will hear: "Have tawakkul in Allāh" when facing difficulties — a reminder that outcomes are in Allāh's hands.
الشِّرْك
Ash-Shirk
Shirk — Associating Partners with Allāh
Shirk is the gravest sin in Islām — associating partners with Allāh in His divinity, worship, or attributes. Allāh says: "Indeed, Allāh does not forgive association with Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills." (al-Nisāʾ 4:48). Allāh does not forgive association with Him for the one who dies upon it — but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills.
You will hear: "Islām came to remove shirk" — referring to the core mission of every prophet.
السُّنَّة
As-Sunnah
Sunnah — The Way of the Prophet ﷺ
The Sunnah refers to everything established from the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ — his words, actions, approvals, and character. It is the second source of Islāmic law after the Qurʾān. Following the Sunnah is commanded in the Qurʾān: "Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it; and whatever he forbids you, abstain from it." (al-Ḥashr 59:7) The Sunnah is understood according to the understanding of the Companions (Ṣaḥābah) — they witnessed its application directly from the Prophet ﷺ.
You will hear: "This is the Sunnah of the Prophet ﷺ" — meaning this practice was established by him.
الرِّزْق
Ar-Rizq
Rizq — Provision from Allāh
Rizq means provision — everything Allāh provides: wealth, health, family, knowledge, time. It is a comprehensive concept. Allāh says: "And there is no creature on earth but that upon Allāh is its provision." (Hūd 11:6). Every person's rizq is decreed by Allāh.
You will hear: "Allāh will provide your rizq" — a reminder that provision comes ultimately from Allāh, not solely from our own efforts.
الفِتْنَة
Al-Fitnah
Fitnah — Trial, Tribulation, or Temptation
Fitnah has several related meanings: a test or trial from Allāh, civil strife or discord, or a temptation that leads one away from the truth. The Qurʾān uses it in all these senses. Allāh says: "And know that your wealth and your children are a fitnah, and that Allāh has with Him a great reward." (al-Anfāl 8:28)
You will hear: "Beware of the fitnah of this time" — referring to the trials and temptations of the modern age.
البَرَكَة
Al-Barakah
Barakah — Divine Blessing
Barakah means divine blessing — an increase and goodness placed by Allāh in something. It is not merely abundance but a blessed quality that causes things to grow and benefit. Barakah is asked for from Allāh alone and comes through acts of worship, gratitude, and following the Sunnah.
You will hear: "May Allāh put barakah in your time/wealth/family" — a duʿāʾ for divine blessing.
الأُمَّة
Al-Ummah
Ummah — The Muslim Community
Ummah refers to the global community of Muslims — all those who follow the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, regardless of race, nationality, or language. Allāh describes this community: "You are the best nation produced for mankind." (Āl ʿImrān 3:110)
You will hear: "The Ummah is going through difficulty" — referring to the global Muslim community.

Worship & Practice ▼

العِبَادَة
Al-ʿIbādah
ʿIbādah — Worship
ʿIbādah means worship — but in Islām, worship is not limited to prayer. It encompasses every action done sincerely for Allāh's sake, including work, raising children, and even smiling at your brother. Ibn Taymiyyah defined ʿibādah as "a comprehensive term covering everything Allāh loves and is pleased with, of words and actions, outward and inward."
You will hear: "The purpose of life is ʿibādah" — referring to the Qurʾānic verse: "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me." (al-Dhāriyāt 51:56)
الدُّعَاء
Ad-Duʿāʾ
Duʿāʾ — Supplication
Duʿāʾ is calling upon Allāh directly — asking Him for anything. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Duʿāʾ is worship." (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3372 — Ḥasan Ṣaḥīḥ) Allāh says: "Call upon Me; I will respond to you." (Ghāfir 40:60). Duʿāʾ can be made at any time, in any language.
You will hear: "Make duʿāʾ for the Ummah" — a call to ask Allāh for the wellbeing of the Muslim community.
الذِّكْر
Adh-Dhikr
Dhikr — Remembrance of Allāh
Dhikr means the remembrance of Allāh through specific phrases, prayers, and recitation of Qurʾān. Allāh says: "Verily, in the remembrance of Allāh do hearts find rest." (al-Raʿd 13:28). Regular dhikr is one of the greatest acts of worship.
You will hear: "Occupy your tongue with dhikr" — meaning keep remembering Allāh throughout the day.
الحَلَال وَالحَرَام
Al-Ḥalāl wa'l-Ḥarām
Ḥalāl and Ḥarām — Permitted and Forbidden
Ḥalāl means that which Allāh has permitted. Ḥarām means that which Allāh has forbidden. These are established by the Qurʾān and authentic Sunnah. Everything not explicitly forbidden is considered permissible by default in Islamic law.
You will hear: "Is this ḥalāl or ḥarām?" — asking whether something is permitted or forbidden in Islām.
الشَّرِيعَة
Ash-Sharīʿah
Sharīʿah — Divine Law
Sharīʿah literally means "the path to water" — the path to life. In Islamic usage it refers to the divine law revealed by Allāh, encompassing worship, ethics, family law, commerce, and all aspects of life. It is derived from the Qurʾān and Sunnah.
You will hear: "The Sharīʿah of Islām" — referring to the complete system of divine guidance.
Section Two

The Five Pillars of Islām

The five pillars are the foundational acts of worship that every Muslim is required to observe. They are established in the famous ḥadīth of Jibrīl and elsewhere in the Qurʾān and Sunnah.

The Prophet ﷺ said: "Islām is built upon five: testifying that there is no god worthy of worship except Allāh and that Muḥammad is the Messenger of Allāh, establishing the prayer, paying the zakāh, fasting Ramaḍān, and performing Ḥajj to the House for whoever is able."
Narrated by Ibn ʿUmar (رضي الله عنهما)
Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 8 · Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 16
First Pillar
Shahādah — The Declaration of Faith
أَشْهَدُ أَن لَّا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ وَأَشْهَدُ أَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا رَّسُولُ ٱللَّهِ
Ashhadu an lā ilāha illā llāh, wa-ashhadu anna Muḥammadan rasūlu llāh
The testimony that there is no god worthy of worship except Allāh alone, and that Muḥammad ﷺ is His final messenger. This is the foundation of the entire Deen — the first thing a person says when entering Islām and the last words a Muslim hopes to say before death.
Second Pillar
Ṣalāh — The Five Daily Prayers
الصَّلَاة
Aṣ-Ṣalāh
The five daily prayers — Fajr (dawn), Ẓuhr (midday), ʿAṣr (afternoon), Maghrib (sunset), ʿIshāʾ (night) — are the most important regular act of worship in Islām. The Prophet ﷺ said it is "the first thing a person will be held accountable for on the Day of Judgement." Ṣalāh was prescribed during the Night Journey (Isrāʾ wal-Miʿrāj).
Third Pillar
Zakāh — The Obligatory Almsgiving
الزَّكَاة
Az-Zakāh
Zakāh is the obligatory annual payment of 2.5% of accumulated wealth above a minimum threshold (niṣāb), distributed to eight categories of recipients specified in the Qurʾān (al-Tawbah 9:60). It purifies wealth and fulfils the rights of the poor. Zakāh is not charity — it is an obligation, one of the pillars of the Deen.
Fourth Pillar
Ṣawm — Fasting in Ramaḍān
صَوْم رَمَضَان
Ṣawm Ramaḍān
Fasting the entire month of Ramaḍān — abstaining from food, drink, and intercourse from dawn to sunset. Allāh says: "O you who believe, fasting has been prescribed upon you as it was prescribed upon those before you, so that you may attain taqwā." (al-Baqarah 2:183). Ramaḍān is also the month the Qurʾān was revealed.
Fifth Pillar
Ḥajj — The Pilgrimage to Makkah
الحَجّ
Al-Ḥajj
The pilgrimage to the Sacred House in Makkah — obligatory once in a lifetime for every Muslim who is physically and financially able. It takes place in the month of Dhū al-Ḥijjah and is the largest annual gathering of humanity. Allāh says: "And Ḥajj to the House is a duty that mankind owes to Allāh — for those who can find a way." (Āl ʿImrān 3:97)
Section Three

The Six Articles of Faith

The six articles of faith (Arkān al-Īmān) are what every Muslim must believe. They are established in the ḥadīth of Jibrīl in which the Prophet ﷺ defined Īmān.

Jibrīl (عليه السلام) asked: "What is Īmān?" The Prophet ﷺ replied: "Īmān is to believe in Allāh, His Angels, His Books, His Messengers, the Last Day, and to believe in divine decree — both its good and its evil."
From the famous ḥadīth of Jibrīl, narrated by ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (رضي الله عنه)
Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8a
📖 Note — Prophet (Nabī) and Messenger (Rasūl)

All Messengers are Prophets, but not all Prophets are Messengers. A Messenger (Rasūl) is sent, scholars explain, with a specific mission to convey revelation to a people, while a Prophet (Nabī) may affirm and teach existing revelation. The precise distinction is discussed in classical scholarship, and Allāh knows best. Both are honoured servants of Allāh. The Qurʾān names twenty-five prophets, among whom scholars identify confirmed messengers. Allāh knows best regarding those whose precise designation is not explicitly specified.

First Article
Belief in Allāh
الإِيمَان بِاللَّه
Belief that Allāh alone is the Creator, Sustainer, and Lord of all that exists — that He alone deserves worship, and that He possesses perfect attributes free from any deficiency or resemblance to creation. This encompasses Tawḥīd — the oneness of Allāh — which is the central message of every prophet. This includes belief in His Names and Attributes as affirmed in the Qurʾān and authentic Sunnah — without distortion (taḥrīf), denial (taʿṭīl), asking how (takyīf), or likening Him to creation (tamthīl).
Second Article
Belief in the Angels
الإِيمَان بِالمَلَائِكَة
Belief that Allāh created angels from light — they are noble beings who worship Allāh constantly, never disobeying Him, and carry out His commands. Among them are Jibrīl (who brought revelation), Mīkāʾīl, Isrāfīl, and the Angel of Death. Two angels — Raqīb and ʿAtīd — record every person's deeds.
Third Article
Belief in the Revealed Books
الإِيمَان بِالكُتُب
Belief that Allāh revealed scriptures to His prophets as guidance for humanity. These include the Ṣuḥuf (scriptures of Ibrāhīm), the Tawrāh (Torah, given to Mūsā), the Zabūr (Psalms, given to Dāwūd), the Injīl (Gospel, given to ʿĪsā), and the Qurʾān — the final, preserved revelation given to Muḥammad ﷺ. Only the Qurʾān remains in its original, unaltered form.
Fourth Article
Belief in the Prophets and Messengers
الإِيمَان بِالأَنْبِيَاء وَالرُّسُل
Belief that Allāh sent prophets to every nation to guide them to worship Him alone. The Qurʾān mentions twenty-five prophets by name. The first was Ādam (عليه السلام) and the last and final prophet is Muḥammad ﷺ. A Muslim must believe in all of them — denying any one of them invalidates faith. Muḥammad ﷺ is the seal of the prophets; there is no prophet after him. This is a definitive matter of creed — denying it removes a person from Islām.
Fifth Article
Belief in the Last Day
الإِيمَان بِاليَوْم الآخِر
Belief in the Day of Resurrection — when all of humanity will be raised from the dead and held accountable before Allāh for every deed. This includes belief in the Hereafter (al-Ākhirah), Paradise (al-Jannah), and Hellfire (al-Nār). Belief in the Last Day gives every action its ultimate weight and purpose.
Sixth Article
Belief in Divine Decree — Al-Qadar
الإِيمَان بِالقَدَر
Belief that Allāh has knowledge of all things before they occur, has written everything in al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ (the Preserved Tablet), that everything happens by His will, and that He is the Creator of all things — including the actions of His creation — while they act with real choice and responsibility, without compulsion or injustice. Nothing occurs outside of Allāh's knowledge and will. Belief in al-Qadar brings tranquillity to the believer: whatever happens was decreed, and Allāh says: "And Allāh is the best of planners." (Āl ʿImrān 3:54)
The Four Levels of Qadar — Sunni Creed
  • ʿIlm — Allāh's eternal knowledge of all things before they occur
  • Kitābah — Allāh wrote everything in al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ (the Preserved Tablet)
  • Mashīʾah — everything occurs by Allāh's will
  • Khalq — Allāh is the Creator of all things, including the actions of His creation — while they act with real choice and responsibility, without compulsion or injustice
Section Four

The Unseen World

Islām teaches that there is a world beyond what our senses perceive — al-Ghayb (the Unseen). Belief in it is part of Īmān. Allāh says: "This is the Book about which there is no doubt, a guidance for those conscious of Allāh — who believe in the unseen." (al-Baqarah 2:2–3)

The Angels — Al-Malāʾikah ▼

Angels are real beings created from light who carry out Allāh's commands throughout creation. They are not male or female, do not eat or drink, and do not disobey Allāh. The Qurʾān describes them as honoured servants: "They do not disobey Allāh in what He commands them but do what they are commanded." (al-Taḥrīm 66:6)

Key Angels Mentioned in the Qurʾān and Sunnah
  • Jibrīl (عليه السلام) — the Angel of Revelation, who brought the Qurʾān to the Prophet ﷺ
  • Mīkāʾīl (عليه السلام) — responsible for rain and sustenance by Allāh's command
  • Isrāfīl (عليه السلام) — will blow the trumpet to signal the Day of Resurrection
  • Malak al-Mawt — The Angel of Death, who takes souls by Allāh's command (al-Sajdah 32:11). Referred to in classical scholarship as ʿIzrāʾīl — this name is not explicitly mentioned in the Qurʾān or authenticated Sunnah.
  • Two recording angels assigned to every person — described in the Qurʾān as raqīb (ever-watchful) and ʿatīd (ready). Scholars differ whether these are proper names or descriptive terms. (Qāf 50:17–18)
  • Munkar and Nakīr — the angels who question the deceased in the grave
  • Mālik — the guardian of Hellfire, mentioned in the Qurʾān (al-Zukhruf 43:77)
  • Riḍwān — mentioned in classical scholarship as the guardian of Paradise. The name Riḍwān is widely cited in classical works; however, its explicit naming is not established in a rigorously authenticated ḥadīth.

The Jinn ▼

The jinn are a creation of Allāh distinct from both humans and angels. Allāh says: "And I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me." (al-Dhāriyāt 51:56) — confirming that jinn, like humans, are accountable beings with free will.

What the Qurʾān and Sunnah establish about Jinn
  • Created from smokeless fire — "And He created the jinn from a smokeless flame of fire." (al-Raḥmān 55:15)
  • They are accountable — they will be judged on the Day of Resurrection
  • Among them are Muslims and disbelievers — Sūrah al-Jinn (72) describes jinn who heard the Qurʾān and accepted Islām
  • They cannot be seen by humans in their natural form
  • Seeking help from jinn or consulting fortune-tellers is forbidden in Islām
  • Protection from harm by jinn is sought through the Qurʾān, especially Āyat al-Kursī (al-Baqarah 2:255) and the last two sūrahs — Sūrah al-Falaq (113) and Sūrah al-Nās (114) — known together as al-Muʿawwidhatān (the two sūrahs of seeking refuge)

Iblīs and Shayṭān ▼

Iblīs was from the jinn who was among the angels in worship, then refused Allāh's command to prostrate to Ādam (عليه السلام) out of arrogance. He became the enemy of humanity. "Indeed, Shayṭān is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy." (Fāṭir 35:6)

Key points established from the Qurʾān
  • Iblīs is from the jinn — not a fallen angel (angels cannot disobey Allāh)
  • He was given respite until the Day of Resurrection and declared his mission: to misguide humanity
  • He has no power to force anyone — he only whispers and suggests
  • He has no power over sincere believers who seek Allāh's refuge
  • Protection: say Aʿūdhu billāhi min al-Shayṭān al-rajīm — "I seek refuge in Allāh from the accursed Shayṭān"
⚠️ Methodological Note — The Unseen

Matters of the unseen (al-Ghayb) are only affirmed as established in the Qurʾān and authentic Sunnah — without speculation, exaggeration, or addition. Where names or details appear only in classical scholarship rather than explicit revelation, this is clearly noted above. Where revelation is silent, we remain silent.

Section Five

Common Islamic Phrases

These phrases are heard constantly in Islamic life. Each carries a meaning rooted in the Qurʾān and Sunnah — understanding them transforms them from words into living acts of worship.

بِسْمِ اللَّه Bismillāh
In the Name of Allāh
Said before beginning any action — eating, drinking, writing, travelling. It places the action under Allāh's blessing and acknowledges that everything begins with His name.
When to say it: Before eating, drinking, entering the home, beginning any task.
الحَمْدُ لِلَّه Alḥamdulillāh
All Praise is Due to Allāh
An expression of gratitude and praise to Allāh — in good times and in difficulty. The Prophet ﷺ said it is the best of duʿāʾ. It opens Sūrah al-Fātiḥah, which is recited in every rakʿah of prayer. The Prophet ﷺ taught us to say it after sneezing.
When to say it: After sneezing, after completing a meal, when asked how you are, upon waking, constantly as an act of gratitude.
اللَّهُ أَكْبَر Allāhu Akbar
Allāh is the Greatest
A declaration that Allāh is greater than everything — greater than our worries, our failures, our circumstances. It is the opening of every prayer (Takbīrat al-Iḥrām) and is called out in the adhān five times a day. It is said in moments of awe, gratitude, and praise.
When to say it: At the start of every prayer, during dhikr, upon seeing something that inspires awe, as part of the takbīr after prayers in Ramaḍān and Eid.
سُبْحَانَ اللَّه SubḥānAllāh
Glory be to Allāh
An expression of Allāh's perfection and transcendence — declaring Him free from all imperfection, deficiency, or resemblance to creation. It is one of the most beloved phrases to Allāh. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Two phrases are light on the tongue, heavy on the scales, beloved to the Most Merciful: SubḥānAllāhi wa-biḥamdih, SubḥānAllāhi l-ʿAẓīm." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)
When to say it: Upon seeing something that inspires wonder, as part of daily dhikr (33 times after each prayer), and when something surprising or unpleasant occurs.
إِن شَاءَ اللَّه InshāʾAllāh
If Allāh Wills
Said when speaking about the future — acknowledging that all future events are in Allāh's hands. It is not a way of saying "maybe" — it is a genuine declaration of dependence on Allāh's will. Allāh commands it in the Qurʾān: "And never say of anything, 'I will do that tomorrow,' except with 'If Allāh wills.'" (al-Kahf 18:23–24)
When to say it: Any time you speak about a future intention or plan.
مَا شَاءَ اللَّه MāshāʾAllāh
What Allāh Has Willed
An expression of appreciation and acknowledgement that something good exists by Allāh's will. It is also said to ward off the evil eye (al-ʿayn) when admiring something or someone. It appears in the Qurʾān (al-Kahf 18:39) where a man is advised to say it when entering his garden.
When to say it: When admiring something or someone, when acknowledging a blessing, to express joy at good news.
جَزَاكَ اللَّهُ خَيْرًا JazākaAllāhu Khayran
May Allāh Reward You with Good
A duʿāʾ of thanks — asking Allāh to give goodness to someone who has done something beneficial. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever has a favour done for him and says JazākaAllāhu Khayran has indeed given the best praise." (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2035 — Ḥasan)
When to say it: In place of or alongside "thank you" when someone helps you or does something kind.
أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّه Astaghfirullāh
I Seek Allāh's Forgiveness
A request for Allāh's forgiveness. The Prophet ﷺ said he sought forgiveness more than seventy times a day despite being forgiven of all sin. It is said after prayer, after making a mistake, or as regular dhikr. Allāh promises: "And whoever does evil or wrongs himself but then seeks forgiveness of Allāh will find Allāh forgiving and merciful." (al-Nisāʾ 4:110)
When to say it: After prayer, after sin or error, as regular dhikr, and whenever conscious of shortcomings.
إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّا إِلَيْهِ رَاجِعُون Innā lillāhi wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn
Indeed We Belong to Allāh and to Him We Shall Return
A Qurʾānic verse (al-Baqarah 2:156) said upon hearing of a death, a loss, or any calamity. It is a statement of profound faith — acknowledging that we and everything we have belongs to Allāh, and that return to Him is certain. The Prophet ﷺ said those who say it with true conviction will receive mercy and guidance from Allāh.
When to say it: Upon hearing of a death, loss, accident, or any difficulty.
السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكُم As-salāmu ʿalaykum
Peace be Upon You
The Islamic greeting — a duʿāʾ of peace upon whoever you meet. The full greeting is As-salāmu ʿalaykum wa-raḥmatullāhi wa-barakātuh — "Peace, mercy, and blessings of Allāh be upon you." The Prophet ﷺ called it one of the best acts: spreading salām among Muslims. It is a right of one Muslim upon another.
When to say it: When meeting another Muslim, entering a gathering, arriving home, and when leaving.
بَارَكَ اللَّهُ فِيكَ BārakAllāhu fīk
May Allāh Bless You
A duʿāʾ of blessing directed at a specific person — asking Allāh to place barakah in them. It is one of the most beloved expressions of gratitude and warmth in Islamic culture. The Prophet ﷺ said that when someone does you a favour, saying JazākaAllāhu Khayran is the best thanks — and BārakAllāhu fīk carries a similar spirit of supplicating for the one who has shown you goodness. The feminine form is BārakAllāhu fīki, and the plural is BārakAllāhu fīkum.
When to say it: When thanking someone, expressing appreciation, or responding to good news or a kind gesture.
اللَّهُمَّ بَارِكْ Allāhumma bārik
O Allāh, Bless
A short duʿāʾ asking Allāh directly to place barakah — in a person, a thing, or a situation. It is often said alongside or instead of MāshāʾAllāh when admiring something, as protection from the evil eye (al-ʿayn). The Prophet ﷺ instructed us that when we see something we admire, we should make duʿāʾ for barakah in it. Saying Allāhumma bārik lahu/lahā (O Allāh, bless him/her) is a Sunnah response to admiration.
When to say it: When admiring a person, child, or blessing — as a protection and a prayer. Often said alongside MāshāʾAllāh.
حَيَّاكُمُ اللَّه Ḥayyākumullāh
May Allāh Give You Life
A warm greeting meaning "May Allāh preserve your life" or "May Allāh grant you a good life" — used when welcoming someone or bidding them farewell. It carries a meaning of honouring and expressing happiness at someone's presence. It is rooted in the Arabic concept of ḥayāh (life), and is an expression of genuine warmth and duʿāʾ for the one being greeted. The singular is Ḥayyāka llāh and the plural is Ḥayyākumullāh.
When to say it: When welcoming guests, greeting someone warmly, or expressing joy at seeing someone.
